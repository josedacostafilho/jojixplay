# Swinging physics: first-hand references and implications

Research date: 2026-09-25. This is design evidence for the `games/swinging` prototype. A bounded elastic pull and timed initial corners are now implemented; repeated cornering and phone feel remain unverified.

## What the developers actually said

- Jamie Fristrom, technical director/designer of the 2004 *Spider-Man 2*, described the goal as pendulum motion attached to buildings, with **the game choosing the anchor** because manually selecting a point made the character go somewhere other than where the player intended. Early prototypes used candidate points at building corners and edges. The team then iterated on anchor search, animation, camera, and street widths/building heights together. [Fristrom's 2004 postmortem, pp. 28–31](https://media.gdcvault.com/GD_Mag_Archives/Game.Developer.2004.09.pdf)
- Fristrom reports that two-web swinging was possible, but the hold-to-swing/release-to-let-go “Easy Swinging” mode was added after unfamiliar players struggled. It made the action understandable immediately. This is a warning against equating strict simulation or extra controls with good feel, especially for our 4–7 audience. [Fristrom's 2004 postmortem, pp. 31–35](https://media.gdcvault.com/GD_Mag_Archives/Game.Developer.2004.09.pdf)
- Insomniac game director Ryan Smith says its later game began with a pendulum and physical building attachments. A line extending sideways makes a turn; a more vertical line supports straighter travel. He identifies **release timing** as central: momentum carries the player farther forward or upward depending on release point. Camera movement and field of view amplify speed, while point selection and traversal transitions protect momentum. [Ryan Smith interview](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/don-t-mean-a-thing-if-you-ain-t-got-that-swing-in-i-spider-man-i-)
- Insomniac's Mike Fitzgerald explicitly says a perfect real-world rope simulation would not produce the desired superhero feel. Their web points were tagged to architecture, selected to preserve momentum and travel direction. [PlayStation developer interview](https://blog.playstation.com/?p=204132)
- For an ideal pendulum, tension acts along the rope and does no work on tangential motion. Gravity converts height to speed toward the low point; speed then converts back to height on the upswing. Thus a dramatic swing needs a real descent, a fast low point, a climb, and an appropriately timed release. [OpenStax University Physics, §8.3](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/8-3-conservation-of-energy)

## First-person VR references

- Sony describes *Far From Home VR* as a first-person, tower-to-tower swinging experience. Its public announcement does not disclose the physics or control algorithm, so it is a presentation reference rather than a source of numerical tuning. [Sony Pictures announcement](https://www.sonypictures.com/corp/press_releases/2019/0701/sonypictureslaunchesspidermantmfarfromhomevirtualreality)
- An independent [open-source WebVR swinging prototype](https://github.com/talkol/spiderman) is more useful technically. Its author uses separate held triggers for two webs, real raycast building hits, and two simultaneous lines. Its [web force implementation](https://github.com/talkol/spiderman/blob/master/web.js) uses a one-sided elastic pull when a line stretches, with part of the force toward a wall removed so a tether does not pin the player to a facade. Initial line length is shorter than the distance to the hit, giving an immediate but bounded pull. The project also offers aiming, a sharp-turn grip, and manual line-length adjustment; those inputs do **not** fit our agreed binary-arm controls. It is an unofficial fan work, not evidence of the official Sony game's internals.
- The VR source suggests testing bounded elastic tension along each real anchor with velocity retained on release. Its wall-force reduction is a gameplay assist, not a necessary property of an elastic web. The owner wants wall avoidance and release timing to remain player skills, so that assist is excluded from our plan. Our existing auto-aim chooses the building point; VR controller pointing is not transferable. [WebVR implementation](https://github.com/talkol/spiderman/blob/master/web.js)

## Diagnosis of the prior hard-rope prototype (inference from its physics code)

The prior solver had fixed world anchors, gravity, a one-sided rope distance constraint, radial-velocity removal when taut, retained velocity on release, and a fixed simulation step. It was deliberately assisted rather than a literal pendulum. The likely source of the owner's “monotonic” feel was that every attached step applied the same forward and side acceleration; every web reeled in continuously; anchor scoring sought nearly the same ahead/lateral geometry; and total speed was hard-capped. Those choices flattened the difference between descending, passing the bottom, climbing, and releasing. This was an inference from code, not a measured playtest result or a documented rule from either commercial game.

A deterministic trace of the current first shot confirms the concern: from 1.0 to 2.5 seconds, the player descends from about 131 m to 114 m while horizontal speed steadily rises from about 24 to 32 m/s. The line remains slack for much of that interval, so no substantial pendulum upswing appears before the nearby building. Trial changes to reel timing and target height produced temporary lift but also broke turning or trapped the player against facades. They were discarded; the rope solver remains unchanged. The later jump pulse adds an independent, timed impulse.

A second disposable trial applied the WebVR-style elastic force and softened pull toward nearby facades. It preserved short turn checks but failed a scripted long circuit because one held line lost forward motion at the next building corner. Rejecting elasticity on that result was a mistake: the script's hold timing belonged to the old solver, and a player should release and reattach to clear the corner. The current prototype uses a bounded one-sided spring without weakening pull toward walls. Tests now measure the observable drop, climb, sideward travel, upward-carry release and one timed 90-degree corner on each side without facade contact. Repeated corners remain to be proved through play.

## Recommendations for our first-person game

1. Keep the two arm states and automatic real-building anchors. Make the *arc* drive speed: gravity should produce a clear dive and fast low point; a release should preserve the current tangent, including its upward component. Record a deterministic one-web trace of height, horizontal/vertical speed, and rope tension so the cycle is visible in data. The research supports this mechanic; it does **not** supply magic gravity or speed constants. [Fristrom](https://media.gdcvault.com/GD_Mag_Archives/Game.Developer.2004.09.pdf), [OpenStax](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/8-3-conservation-of-energy)
2. The prototype now uses bounded elastic tension with automatic real-building anchors and full wall collision; constant forward push and perpetual reeling are removed. Compare deliberate early and late releases and improve cornering before changing anchor selection or adding collision assists. The cited games do not disclose force constants for our setup. [WebVR implementation](https://github.com/talkol/spiderman/blob/master/web.js), [Ryan Smith](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/don-t-mean-a-thing-if-you-ain-t-got-that-swing-in-i-spider-man-i-)
3. The binary jump pulse now adds bounded impulses from roof/wall contact and taut webs without automatically releasing a held arm. This is our game design, not an established mechanic from the cited games. Its strength and timing remain provisional until phone play.
4. Let the first-person view communicate momentum without obscuring heading: restrained pitch into a dive/climb, a small speed-dependent field-of-view change, and stable travel-direction yaw. Keep these visual effects subordinate to the actual trajectory; the prior reversed-heading bug showed why orientation matters. [Fristrom](https://media.gdcvault.com/GD_Mag_Archives/Game.Developer.2004.09.pdf), [Ryan Smith](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/don-t-mean-a-thing-if-you-ain-t-got-that-swing-in-i-spider-man-i-)
5. Validate feel with traces and fresh players: compare early/late release, alternate arms in a straight street, one-side cornering, dual-web transitions, and recovery near the ground. On-phone play by the owner is necessary because browser trajectory tests cannot establish comfort, clarity or delight. Fristrom's report is unusually clear that expert demonstrations masked novice frustration. [Fristrom](https://media.gdcvault.com/GD_Mag_Archives/Game.Developer.2004.09.pdf)

The publicly readable 2004 postmortem gives the design history but no exact integrator, force constants, or source code. The [2019 GDC session page](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1025725/Classic-Game-Design-Postmortem-Swinging) confirms a deeper first-hand talk exists, but the accessible page contains only its abstract; this note does not infer numerical settings from it. Later Insomniac systems are informative analogies, not claims about the 2004 implementation.
